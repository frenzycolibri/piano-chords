Cheatsheet

How chords are built

A half step is the very next key, black or white. A full step is two half steps.

A chord is a root note plus notes a fixed number of half steps above it. The formula never changes with the key: C7 is always C – E – G – B♭. The key only decides whether a chord fits its scale.

Finding the 7th quickly

Go to the root one octave up, then step down:

  • maj7: down a half step. C → B gives Cmaj7.
  • 7, m7, m7♭5: down a full step. C → B♭ gives C7.
  • dim7: down three half steps. C → B𝄫 (the A key) gives Cdim7.

Chords in a major key

Scale steps: full · full · half · full · full · full · half. On C: C – D – E – F – G – A – B – C.

Stacking every other note of a major scale always gives the same pattern. In C major:

Degree1234567
TriadsCDmEmFGAmBdim
7th chordsCmaj7Dm7Em7Fmaj7G7Am7Bm7♭5

1 and 4 are major, 2, 3 and 6 are minor, 5 is the only dominant 7th and 7 is diminished.

Chords in a minor key

Scale steps: full · half · full · full · half · full · full. On C: C – D – E♭ – F – G – A♭ – B♭ – C.

The natural minor scale uses the same notes as the major scale three half steps above it, starting from its 6th degree. In C minor, which shares its notes with E♭ major:

Degree1234567
TriadsCmDdimE♭FmGmA♭B♭
7th chordsCm7Dm7♭5E♭maj7Fm7Gm7A♭maj7B♭7

1, 4 and 5 are minor, 3, 6 and 7 are major, 7 is the only dominant 7th and 2 is diminished.

Chords in a harmonic minor key

Scale steps: full · half · full · full · half · 3 half steps · half. On C: C – D – E♭ – F – G – A♭ – B – C.

Harmonic minor raises the 7th of the natural minor scale by a half step. In C harmonic minor B♭ becomes B:

Degree1234567
TriadsCmDdimE♭augFmGA♭Bdim
7th chordsCm(maj7)Dm7♭5E♭maj7♯5Fm7G7A♭maj7Bdim7

Every chord containing the 7th changes: 1 gets a major 7th, 3 becomes augmented, 5 becomes major with a dominant 7th and 7 becomes diminished. 2, 4 and 6 stay the same.

Key signatures

  • Sharps always appear in the order F – C – G – D – A – E – B. The major key is a half step above the last sharp: F♯ C♯ → D major.
  • Flats always appear in the order B – E – A – D – G – C – F. The major key is the second-to-last flat: B♭ E♭ A♭ → E♭ major. One flat is F major.
  • Minor: the minor key with the same signature is three half steps below the major key: E♭ major → C minor.

Major and minor share a signature, so look at the first note of the scale to tell them apart. Harmonic minor uses the minor key's signature plus its raised 7th, written as an accidental on the 7th note: a ♯, ♮ or 𝄪 on the last note of the scale means harmonic minor.

Inversions

Move the lowest note up an octave to get the next inversion. Chords are stacked thirds (3 or 4 half steps), so moving a note up leaves one wider gap of a fourth (5 half steps). Find that gap to find the root: the root is always the note just above it.

InversionNotesGapsShapeRoot
Root positionC – E – G3rd · 3rdEvenly spaced, like a snowmanBottom
1st inversionE – G – C3rd · 4thWide gap at the topTop
2nd inversionG – C – E4th · 3rdWide gap at the bottomMiddle

7th chords have four notes, so inverting them leaves a step (a 2nd, 1 or 2 half steps) instead of a gap. The root is the upper note of that step:

InversionNotesGapsShapeRoot
Root positionC – E – G – B♭3rd · 3rd · 3rdEvenly spacedBottom
1st inversionE – G – B♭ – C3rd · 3rd · 2ndStep at the topTop
2nd inversionG – B♭ – C – E3rd · 2nd · 3rdStep in the middleUpper middle
3rd inversionB♭ – C – E – G2nd · 3rd · 3rdStep at the bottomLower middle

The trick does not work for aug, dim7, sus2 and sus4: their inversions have no single odd gap, so count from the note names instead.
